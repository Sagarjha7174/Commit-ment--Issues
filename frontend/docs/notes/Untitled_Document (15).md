# Green Home Score: 24 user personas and the hidden problems they reveal

The easiest way to design a bad housing product is to imagine one perfectly informed homeowner who owns a detached house, has spare money, never moves, gets every permission approved, and uses the same appliances forever. An impressive creature, presumably raised in a spreadsheet.

We need to design for actual people, whose incentives conflict, information is incomplete, and circumstances change.

I'd approach this through personas, day-to-day situations, and failure patterns. The objective isn't to create 24 separate products. It's to uncover the distinct decisions the same underlying system must support.

These personas are illustrative design profiles, not statistical claims about how common each group is.

## 2. The important patterns that personas alone won't reveal

The personas tell us who uses the product. The patterns below reveal where the product can fail even when it understands the user correctly.

25\. The bill doesn't match the model

Scenario: The system estimates £120 per month, but the household pays £190.

Why it matters: Tariffs, standing charges, billing periods, estimated readings, occupancy, and appliance loads can all explain the difference.

Product requirement: Separate predicted energy consumption from predicted bills. Let users compare against actual bills and investigate discrepancies instead of silently recalibrating the model.

26\. The household wants comfort, not minimum consumption

Scenario: Insulation improves, but the occupants use the improvement to keep the home warmer in winter or cooler in summer.

Why it matters: Energy savings may be lower than engineering estimates because people change their behaviour. This is often called the rebound effect.

Product requirement: Show separate outcomes for fixed comfort assumptions and realistic changes in comfort behaviour. Never promise the full engineering saving as guaranteed bill savings.

27\. Seasonal and daily usage patterns

Scenario: A home is empty during working hours, occupied all day on weekends, and uses substantial cooling during summer.

Why it matters: Annual averages conceal peak demand, time-of-use tariffs, and when energy is actually consumed.

Product requirement: Allow seasonal and occupancy schedules. Add hourly modelling only when data and the use case justify it.

28\. The property data is wrong or outdated

Scenario: An EPC record describes an old boiler, but the owner replaced it two years ago. The floor area or insulation details may also be wrong.

Why it matters: A precise calculation based on incorrect inputs is still incorrect. Computers have yet to develop shame about this.

Product requirement: Display the source and age of important data, allow user corrections, distinguish verified from inferred inputs, and recalculate after changes.

29\. Retrofit measures depend on each other

Scenario: A heat pump is installed before the heating system and building fabric are assessed. The result may be more expensive or less effective than expected.

Why it matters: Some measures require enabling work, change the value of later measures, or should be installed in a particular order.

Product requirement: Represent dependencies and installation sequencing. Compare complete pathways, not merely independent measure scores.

30\. The upgrade is installed but not maintained

Scenario: A ventilation system's filters are never replaced, controls are configured incorrectly, or an installation performs below specification.

Why it matters: Real performance changes over time, and maintenance has costs.

Product requirement: Include maintenance intervals, expected replacement costs, warranties, and performance degradation where reliable evidence exists. Allow post-installation verification.

31\. The recommended installer or equipment is unavailable

Scenario: The recommendation is sensible, but local installers are booked for months, a component is unavailable, or the property needs electrical upgrades first.

Why it matters: A technically optimal package may not be actionable now.

Product requirement: Eventually account for local contractor availability, lead times, installation capacity, and comparable alternatives. Treat live availability as sourced, time-sensitive data.

32\. Installation disruption makes a good option impractical

Scenario: Work requires the household to leave for several days, creates dust, blocks access, or interrupts heating or hot water.

Why it matters: Installation cost is not the only cost. Disruption is especially important for families, older occupants, and people working from home.

Product requirement: Show expected installation duration, disruption, temporary relocation needs, and the availability of staged work when this information is known.

33\. Energy supply and electrical infrastructure are constrained

Scenario: The home has limited electrical capacity, unreliable power, a shared heating system, or restrictions on exporting electricity.

Why it matters: Equipment ratings and theoretical energy yields do not establish that an installation can work as intended.

Product requirement: Check electrical capacity, grid-connection requirements, existing system compatibility, and outage behaviour where relevant. Escalate unknown technical conditions for inspection.

34\. The incentive or regulation changes

Scenario: A grant expires, eligibility rules change, a tariff changes, or new requirements apply to a particular property.

Why it matters: A recommendation that was valid six months ago may no longer be financially or legally valid.

Product requirement: Date-stamp regulatory and incentive information, identify the jurisdiction, provide source links, and distinguish current verified rules from outdated or unverified information.

35\. Household members disagree

Scenario: One person wants a warmer home, another wants lower bills, and another controls the household budget.

Why it matters: There may be no single objective called “the user's preference.”

Product requirement: Support multiple priorities, explain trade-offs, and make the chosen objective explicit. For shared buildings, distinguish the committee's decision from each resident's preferences.

36\. A seemingly green upgrade creates a safety or building problem

Scenario: Insulation is added without addressing moisture, ventilation is inadequate, or an appliance installation creates a safety issue.

Why it matters: Carbon savings cannot compensate for unsafe indoor conditions or damage to the building.

Product requirement: Make safety and building-physics constraints hard gates, not small penalties in a weighted score. Require qualified assessment for conditions the model cannot establish.

37\. A user can afford the upgrade but not the timing

Scenario: A homeowner can afford a £10,000 project in principle but needs the cash for another essential expense this year.

Why it matters: Total affordability, monthly affordability, and available cash today are different constraints.

Product requirement: Model upfront costs, payment timing, ongoing costs, financing assumptions, and an optional financial buffer. Avoid presenting financing as automatically beneficial.

38\. The user changes their mind halfway through

Scenario: A tenant decides to move, a homeowner postpones solar, or the budget is reduced after receiving contractor quotes.

Why it matters: The original recommendation may no longer be appropriate.

Product requirement: Support saved scenarios, editable assumptions, versioned comparisons, and recalculation without losing the previous decision. Explain exactly what changed and why the ranking changed.

39\. The user does not want to share sensitive information

Scenario: A person wants personalised advice but does not want to disclose their salary, employment details, exact energy bills, or occupancy schedule.

Why it matters: Excessive data collection creates privacy risk and makes the product harder to use.

Product requirement: Use minimum necessary inputs, accept ranges or user-selected financial scenarios, and clearly state when less information reduces confidence.

## 3. More patterns that emerge when the system is used over time

The previous cases expose individual failure modes. These additional patterns affect the entire product.

| Pattern                            | What Green Home Score needs to handle                                                                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Changing energy prices             | Compare results under alternative tariffs and future price assumptions rather than treating today's prices as permanent.            |
| Carbon intensity changes           | Distinguish energy savings from emissions savings when grid electricity becomes cleaner or dirtier.                                 |
| Extreme weather                    | Stress-test performance during heatwaves, cold spells, and locally relevant hazards.                                                |
| Equipment end-of-life              | Coordinate upgrades with the natural replacement cycle of boilers, windows, appliances, and roofs.                                  |
| Existing debt or commitments       | Let users account for ongoing payments without requiring detailed financial records.                                                |
| Insurance and warranties           | Flag potential implications of modifications and the need to verify coverage with the relevant provider.                            |
| Neighbour and shared-wall effects  | Account for shared structures, noise, access, permissions, and benefits that cross property boundaries.                             |
| Actual versus promised performance | Compare post-installation energy use with the original estimate, accounting for weather and occupancy changes.                      |
| Multiple occupants and privacy     | Avoid exposing one household member's usage or financial inputs to another without appropriate permission.                          |
| Accessibility and language         | Provide understandable explanations, alternative input methods, and actionable summaries rather than requiring technical expertise. |
| Local service ecosystem            | Distinguish recommendations that are locally supportable from those that depend on unavailable expertise or equipment.              |
| Moving equipment between homes     | Identify which upgrades are portable, which remain with the property, and whether moving them is economical or permitted.           |

## 4. The deeper product insight: every recommendation needs a lifecycle

A recommendation should not be just:

> Install measure X. It saves Y kWh per year.

It should eventually answer six questions:

1. Can I do it? Physical, legal, technical, and permission feasibility.
2. Can I afford it now? Upfront costs, timing, financing, and alternatives.
3. Will it work for my circumstances? Climate, occupancy, building type, and usage patterns.
4. What happens if things change? Relocation, new occupants, appliances, tariffs, and unexpected events.
5. How do I know the result is real? Data provenance, uncertainty, measured outcomes, and verification.
6. What happens after installation? Maintenance, replacement, future compatibility, and changing regulations.

That gives us a reusable structure across all 39 profiles without building 39 independent recommendation systems.

## 5. What matters most for the first version

A warning before this becomes a magnificent catalogue of features that never ships: we should not attempt to implement every pattern at once.

Essential

Make recommendations trustworthy

Property data quality, explicit assumptions, tenant/owner roles, hard feasibility constraints, cost and energy calculations, missing-data handling, and transparent uncertainty.

Next

Make recommendations practical

Occupancy horizon, upfront budget, seasonal usage, measure dependencies, staged upgrades, maintenance, and a small set of future scenarios.

Later

Expand the decision ecosystem

Live contractor availability, portfolio management, automated bill reconciliation, detailed hourly simulations, ongoing monitoring, financing integrations, and multi-party coordination.

For the Lloyds challenge specifically, the scope should remain anchored to the UK data, geography, and rules supported by the existing plan. A product architecture that can eventually support other countries is sensible; pretending to validate UK EPC recommendations, Indian apartment-society rules, and multiple countries' financial incentives in one hackathon is not.

My strongest recommendation: design Green Home Score around a decision record that stores the property facts, user objectives, assumptions, constraints, chosen measures, estimated outcomes, and model/data versions. That makes it possible to revisit a recommendation after a move, a new appliance, a price change, or a failed installation and explain why the answer changed.

That is the foundation that turns this from a one-time calculator into a product people can use throughout the life of a property.
